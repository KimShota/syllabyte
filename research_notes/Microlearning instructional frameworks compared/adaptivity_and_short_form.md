# Adaptivity (learner-level tailoring) and engagement/attention design for short-form educational video

Evidence-quality legend used below: [M] = meta-analysis, [RCT/Exp] = controlled experiment, [Obs] = observational/log data, [Sec] = I only saw a secondary summary (verify before citing numerically), [Single] = single study or small sample. Most numeric values below were retrieved via search summaries or citing papers, not the paper full text (publisher sites returned 403). Anything marked [Sec] should be checked against the original DOI before it goes into a final report.

## Q1. Does tailoring lesson structure by prior knowledge (worked example for novices, problem-solving/"what-if" for advanced) have solid evidence, and what are effect sizes?

### Takeaway
The *principle* is well supported: a 2025 meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect (60 studies, 176 effects) finds high-support instruction helps low-prior-knowledge learners (d ~ +0.5) and low-support instruction helps high-prior-knowledge learners (d ~ -0.43 for high support). The *specific* mapping "worked example for L1 / key steps for L2 / what-if for L3" is plausible and consistent with worked-example-fading research, but no source I found tested that exact 3-way short-video design. Adaptive instruction in general (ITS, adaptive systems) shows moderate positive effects, but effects shrink with standardized tests and strict controls, and evidence that adaptation per se beats non-adaptive instruction is mixed.

### Cited Findings

**Expertise reversal effect (core evidence)**
- Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025), "A cornerstone of adaptivity – A meta-analysis of the expertise reversal effect," Learning and Instruction 98, 102142 [M]. Per a blog summary: 60 studies, 176 effect sizes, almost 6,000 participants; novices learn more from high-support than low-support pedagogy (d = 0.505); experts learn more from low-support than high-support pedagogy (d = -0.428); effect grows with age and is strongest in higher education; smaller in language/humanities. Explanations offered: cognitive load (extra support becomes extraneous load for experts) and self-determination theory (support can reduce experts' autonomy). The blog cautions that prior knowledge and structure are continuous, so the results do not translate into step-by-step rules. [Learning & the Brain summary (secondary)](https://www.learningandthebrain.com/blog/one-size-doesnt-fit-all-teaching-experts-and-novices/); bibliographic record: [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142). Higher education (the user's population) is where the effect was reported as strongest. [Sec]
- Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017), Educational Psychology Review 29(2), 393-405, argue the expertise reversal effect is a particular case of the element interactivity effect (conceptual, not a meta-analysis). [Search result record](https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s11251-012-9237-2) (listing returned alongside; the paper itself was not opened) [Sec]

**Worked examples: does the benefit depend on prior knowledge?**
- Barbieri et al. (2023), Educational Psychology Review, meta-analysis of 55 studies on math learning: worked examples gave a medium benefit over problem solving alone (university summary: about 18 percentile points), for both initial skill acquisition and practice. The blog summary states the authors did not find that the benefit consistently depended on prior knowledge (some studies larger for high prior knowledge, some for low, some no interaction), and a poster from the team says none of the tested moderators was significant. [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1); [UD news](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/in-the-news-christina-barbieri/); [UD poster](https://www.cehd.udel.edu/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Worked-Examples-Meta-Poster_022722_SC.pdf) [M, Sec]. This partially cuts against a clean "worked example only for novices" claim at the meta-analytic level.
- Contrast: the older Crissman (2006) dissertation meta-analysis reported novices benefited more than students with prior knowledge. [Learning Scientists blog](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2024/1/25-1) mentioned via the same search summary [Sec, dissertation]

**Kalyuga's rapid assessment and adaptive fading**
- Kalyuga & Sweller (2004, J. Educational Psychology 96, "Measuring knowledge to optimize cognitive load factors during instruction") and (2005, ETR&D 53, "Rapid dynamic assessment of expertise to improve the efficiency of adaptive e-learning") used rapid diagnostic methods (first-step method, rapid verification) to place learners on a worked example / faded example / problem-solving continuum in adaptive tutorials (linear equations, kinematics); the adaptive group reportedly showed superior learning. [IGI chapter summarizing the line](https://igi-global.com/chapter/adaptive-procedures-efficient-learning/25741); [Kalyuga search result (Heriot-Watt record)](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/the-expertise-reversal-effect-and-worked-examples-in-tutored-prob/) [Sec; effect sizes not retrieved]
- Fading worked examples adaptively (by student performance) appears more beneficial than fixed fading (Salden, Aleven, Schwonke & Renkl, 2010, Instructional Science, compared Cognitive Tutor with fixed vs adaptive fading); Renkl, Atkinson & Grosse (2004) is the foundational fading reference. [Search summary, same Heriot-Watt record](https://researchportal.hw.ac.uk/en/publications/the-expertise-reversal-effect-and-worked-examples-in-tutored-prob/) [Sec, single studies]
- The rapid-assessment tasks were short (seconds to ~1 min per item), which is the relevant fact for an IRT-based placement upstream of short videos. [IGI chapter](https://igi-global.com/chapter/adaptive-procedures-efficient-learning/25741) [Sec]

**Intelligent tutoring systems (ITS)**
- Kulik & Fletcher (2016), Review of Educational Research 86(1), 42-78: 50 controlled evaluations; median effect 0.66 SD (50th to 75th percentile). Effect 0.73 on local (researcher-made) tests vs 0.13 on standardized tests; authors conclude effects were small once control treatments and implementation adequacy were accounted for. [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.3102/0034654315581420); [IDA report](https://www.ida.org/research-and-publications/publications/all/e/ef/effectiveness-of-intelligent-tutoring-systems-a-meta-analytic-review) [M, Sec]. A 2025 arXiv meta-analysis cites the same review as Glass's delta 0.44 for K-12 combined, so exact figures vary by subset. [arXiv 2511.04997](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.04997)
- VanLehn (2011), Educational Psychologist 46(4): human tutoring about d = 0.79 vs no tutoring; step-based ITS about 0.76; substep-based about 0.40; VanLehn's own summary: human and (step-based) computer tutors have similar effects around 0.75, far below Bloom's 2.0. [ERIC record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ946764); [secondary summary](https://marginalrevolution.com/marginalrevolution/2013/10/how-good-are-computers-as-tutors.html) [M-style review, Sec]
- Bloom's "2 sigma": Bloom (1984) reported about 1 sigma for mastery learning under good conditions and up to 2 sigma for tutoring plus mastery; commentary notes the 2-sigma figure was an illustration and later estimates are smaller (mastery learning reviews around 0.5-1.0 SD; Nickow, Oreopoulos & Quan 2020 tutoring meta-analysis 0.37 SD; Cohen, Kulik & Kulik 1982 tutoring about 0.33). [Wikipedia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloom%27s_2_sigma_problem); [Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/two-sigma-tutoring-separating-science-fiction-from-science/); [Slavin 1987 (via gwern)](https://Www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/1987-slavin.pdf) [Sec]
- Scaffolding: Belland, Walker, Kim & Lefler (2017), RER 87(2), meta-analysis of computer-based scaffolding in STEM: overall g = 0.46 (as cited by a later Educational Psychology Review paper); the same group's pilot reported g = 0.53. [Springer citing paper](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/S10648-017-9419-1); [pilot summary](https://cadrek12.org/resources/pilot-meta-analysis-computer-based-scaffolding-stem-education) [M, Sec]

**Adaptive vs non-adaptive in general**
- A 2024 meta-analysis (45 studies) of AI-enabled adaptive learning vs non-adaptive approaches reports a medium-to-large positive effect on cognitive outcomes (exact g not retrieved). [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/07356331241240459) [M, Sec]
- Adaptivity in educational games: no overall advantage of adaptive conditions (g = .11, p = .26); positive for learning outcomes (g = .39) but negative for game performance (g = -.27). [HKU record](https://hub.hku.hk/handle/10722/318846) [M, Sec]
- Personalized/adaptive technology in LMICs (Major et al., 2021): overall 0.18; approaches that adjust to learner level 0.35. [EdTech Hub](https://edtechhub.org/?p=61468) [M, Sec]

### Inferences
- Level-based tailoring has the strongest theoretical and meta-analytic backing for a coarse dichotomy (more guidance for low prior knowledge, less for high). Expect d about 0.4-0.5 *in the crossover direction* for well-matched vs mismatched structure, but this is an average of heterogeneous manipulations, not a test of "worked example vs what-if".
- The strongest mechanistic reading for CLT-bLM: L1 worked example = high support (supported); L3 "what-if" question = low-support/generative prompt (consistent with direction of the effect, but "what-if" in a 60-90 s video is a minimal, ungraded prompt, so it likely behaves more like a transfer/curiosity prompt than true problem solving).
- Since Barbieri finds no reliable prior-knowledge moderation for worked examples vs problems, do not over-claim: the benefit of the L1 design may come mainly from worked examples being good for everyone in procedural content, and the L3 design needs to be justified by the expertise-reversal (guidance overload/redundancy), not by "worked examples hurt experts".
- Kalyuga's adaptive fading suggests a design upgrade: rather than three fixed variants, route learners by the IRT level but also fade within a concept sequence (L1 full example -> L2 partially completed steps -> L3 only the problem/what-if). That would be a hypothesis, not a validated short-video result.
- ITS effect sizes (0.4-0.8) mostly come from step-level feedback and practice, which a one-way 60-90 s video cannot deliver; do not transfer those effect sizes to the video itself. The video benefits come from the *placement/adaptation*, not tutoring.

### Gaps
- Original-paper effect sizes for Kalyuga's adaptive-vs-non-adaptive comparisons (not retrieved; only abstracts/secondary text).
- No study found testing worked-example vs problem-first vs what-if as level-specific variants of the *same concept* in video format.
- Tetzlaff et al. exact CIs, heterogeneity, and any moderator for multimedia/video format could not be read (ScienceDirect 403).
- Knowledge tracing (BKT/DKT) literature was not searched; no findings recorded.
- Zone of proximal development / Vygotskian scaffolding fading: only the computer-based scaffolding meta-analysis was found; no evidence specific to video scaffolds.

## Q2. Value (or harm) of an attention hook in short educational video for learning vs engagement; seductive details

### Takeaway
No rigorous study isolating the first 5-15 seconds of an educational video was found; the "hook in the first N seconds" claims are mostly industry/marketing lore. The strongest related evidence is (a) seductive-details meta-analyses showing interesting-but-irrelevant material hurts learning modestly (g about -0.2 to -0.35, with disputes), and (b) emotional/personal design meta-analyses showing small positive effects when the design is integrated with the content. A hook is therefore likely safe and useful if it is content-relevant (a question or problem about the concept), and risky if it is decorative.

### Cited Findings

**Hook / first seconds**
- A search for experimental evidence on opening seconds of educational video returned no rigorous study; claims of "8 seconds" or "first 15 seconds" are from marketing blogs and school-site advice with no clear primary source. [Delta State summary](https://www.deltastate.edu/fordctl/wp-content/uploads/sites/71/2019/09/Viewer-Retention-in-Videos.pdf); [NCSSM](https://www.ncssm.edu/stories/create-more-engaging-educational-videos-with-a-few-best-practices); [Virlo (marketing)](https://virlo.ai/blog/why-the-first-few-seconds-matter) [Obs/lore, low quality]
- A study of coronary patients' eLearning videos found videos under 100 s were typically watched without pausing; longer ones were stopped early or rewound. [Search result (F1000Research article 8-705)](https://f1000research.com/articles/8-705) [Single, Sec]

**Seductive details**
- Rey (2012) meta-analysis: 39 experimental effects; medium negative effects on retention and transfer, larger with limited study time and with visual seductive details (reported g ranges around -0.27 to -0.95 retention, -0.65 to -0.83 transfer in one secondary summary). [Search summary: e-teaching/ACU](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/seductive-details) [M, Sec]
- Sundararajan & Adesope (2020), "Keep it Coherent," Educational Psychology Review 32, 707-734: 58 studies, >7,500 students, 68 effects; small-to-moderate negative effect (g = -0.33 in one secondary page); worse when details are static, on paper, backtracking allowed, and with no prior knowledge; no moderation by format, timing, size, placement, or frequency in one umbrella summary. [e-teaching.org](https://www.e-teaching.org/materialien/literatur/sundararajan-adesope-2020); [WSU news](https://news.wsu.edu/2020/03/19/seductive-details-inhibit-learning/); [QIC-WD umbrella summary](https://www.qic-wd.org/sites/default/files/Umbrella%20Summary%20-%20Seductive%20Details%20in%20Training%20041821.pdf) [M, partly Sec]
- A multilevel meta-analysis (177 effect sizes from 50 studies) reported a smaller effect (g = -0.16), strongest on comprehension, then recall and transfer; and some replication-style work found no consistent harm of high-interest details. [Springer record (Instructional Science 2023)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-023-09632-w) (attribution of the g = -0.16 figure comes from the search summary only) [M, Sec]. Interpretation in recent work: "diversion" rather than disruption of attention may be the mechanism. [same search summary]
- Brame (2016): "weeding" (removing interesting but non-goal-serving material), signaling, segmenting, and matching audio/visual are core video-design principles; active processing is needed because passive viewing can be as unproductive as reading. [PMC5132380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/) [review/guidelines, not new data]

**Emotional design and personalization (engagement without seduction)**
- Wong & Adesope (2021), Educational Psychology Review 33, 357-385, meta-analysis (replicating Brom et al., 2018): 28 studies for retention, 38 transfer, 16 comprehension; positive overall; a citing paper reports g = 0.35 retention, 0.27 transfer, 0.29 comprehension; effects on affect were weaker than on learning. [NYU IFP abstract](https://ifp.nyu.edu/2020/journal-article-abstracts/s10648-020-09545-x/); [Frontiers citing paper](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.797236/pdf) [M, Sec on g values]
- Ginns, Martin & Marsh (2013), Educational Psychology Review 25, 445-472, conversational style/personalization meta-analysis: retention d = 0.30, transfer d = 0.54 (3,312 students); lower perceived difficulty; no clear interest gain (d about 0.15, not verified). [Western Sydney record](https://researchers.westernsydney.edu.au/en/publications/designing-instructional-text-in-a-conversational-style-a-meta-ana/) [M, Sec]
- Robustness caveat: a 2024 study found personalization alone did not improve learning unless other factors intensified task involvement. [Almeida et al. 2024, Computer Assisted Learning](https://madoc.bib.uni-mannheim.de/67483/1/Computer%20Assisted%20Learning%20-%202024%20-%20Almeida%20-%20More%20personal%20but%20not%20better%20The%20personalization%20effect%20in%20learning%20neutral.pdf) [Single]
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014): informal, personal-feel videos, talking head alternating with slides, and enthusiastic fast speakers (higher engagement at faster speaking rates) gave higher engagement; this is engagement (watch time, problem attempts), not learning. [Paper PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf) [Obs]

### Inferences
- Treat the "Prepare/hook" stage as a *content-relevant* question, conflict, or counter-intuitive claim about the concept (what Brame calls weeding-compatible); avoid decorative humor, unrelated stories, flashy visuals. The seductive-detail meta-analyses imply a cost of roughly g -0.2 to -0.35 for irrelevant interesting material; emotional design that is integrated with content (e.g., warm colors/faces on instructional elements) shows a positive g about 0.3.
- A hook that doubles as a prequestion could add learning benefit (see Q4), not just attention. This is an inference; direct evidence for hooks-as-prequestions in short video was not found.
- Tight 60-90 s length is well within the Guo engagement regime (0-3 min videos had the highest engagement, 75% of sessions over three-quarters of the length); but Guo's finding is for MOOC lectures on edX, not phone feeds.
- Conversational/personal narration has modest but consistent support (g about 0.3-0.5); cheap to add.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed experiment on opening-seconds hook design in educational video found.
- Specific effect of vertical format, captions/on-screen text (redundancy/split attention) in phone-sized screens not found in this search pass (Mayer's multimedia principles were not retrieved with numbers).
- Original text of Rey (2012) and Sundararajan & Adesope (2020) not accessed; g values come from secondary pages and conflict (-0.33 vs -0.16 vs ranges).
- "Hibbert" source in the assignment was not located; I could not verify what it refers to.

## Q3. Best-supported structures for short vertical videos aimed at learning; what do educational TikTok/Shorts/Reels studies find?

### Takeaway
Direct evidence on vertical, feed-delivered educational video is thin and contradictory: well-structured short lesson clips can match or beat a long lecture on immediate quizzes in small studies, while fMRI/behavioral experiments on fragmented short clips in a feed-like format show poorer memory accuracy and faster forgetting. The consistent message is that coherence (a single, well-structured narrative unit) matters more than length, and delayed retention is unproven.

### Cited Findings
- Nature npj Science of Learning (2025), "Fragmented learning from short videos modulates neural activity and connectivity during memory retrieval": 57 participants watched one continuous video or multiple short clips of equal total duration/content; the short-clip group recalled less accurately and showed weaker narrative reconstruction at retrieval (fMRI). [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41539-025-00399-y); coverage with caveat that the stimuli were fragmented travel clips, not lessons: [ScienceBlog](https://scienceblog.com/t-short-form-videos-may-make-newly-learned-information-harder-to-remember-but-the-experiment-tested-fragmented-travel-clips-rather-than-every-brief-lesson-or-every-persons-scrolling-habit/) [Exp, small N]
- Nature Communications Psychology (2026), "Learning via short videos impairs memory accuracy and reduces brain synchrony": short-video learning gave lower recall accuracy and higher forgetting rate under incidental and intentional memory conditions (details from search summary only). [Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/s44271-026-00476-x) [Exp, Sec]
- "The Reel Deal: Designing and Evaluating LLM-Generated Short-Form Educational Videos" (ACM, 2025): 62 students randomly assigned to LLM-generated short reels vs original long lecture; quiz scores 93.85% vs 79.72% (p = 0.0001), faster completion; only immediate recall measured; custom navigable interface (not a standard feed); no human-curated short-form or AI-processed long-form control. [ACM](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3749012.3749048); [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.05962) [Single, small N, immediate test]
- INTED2026 conference paper (NC State): 68 online-course students randomized to four 1-minute videos vs one 8-minute video, mixed-methods; results not visible in the retrieved snippet. [IATED](https://library.iated.org/view/MCKEOWN2026DES) [Single]
- Reported experiment (123 participants, via Spektrum der Wissenschaft as relayed by Galaxus): video-form information was memorized less well than text; watching entertainment video beforehand did not measurably reduce learning but tended toward superficial thinking. [Galaxus](https://www.galaxus.ch/en/page/short-videos-encourage-shallow-thinking-40485) (machine-translated secondary report, original not located) [Single, Sec]
- Small exploratory eye-tracking study (16 young viewers, 2026): short videos are not a uniform processing format; format and content type shape attention allocation. [iMotions](https://imotions.com/blog/publications/watching-tiktok-an-eye-tracking-study-of-visual-attention-paid-to-short-form-videos/) [Single, N=16]
- Randomized web experiment (N = 435, ages 13-24): short-form informed-consent videos gave comprehension similar to written consent; TikTok videos averaged 32-42 s in Aug 2022-Jan 2023. [PMC11624449](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11624449/) [RCT, non-educational content]
- Cross-sectional pharmacology study of 5-7 minute summary videos: students *perceived* better retention; self-report only. [PMC12752264](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12752264/) [Single, perception]
- Microlearning meta-analysis (higher education): five studies, microlearning post-test mean difference +12.6 points (95% CI 1.2 to 23.9); microlearning is a broader construct than short video. [Senadheera et al. 2024, JMTR](https://journals.kln.ac.lk/jmtr/media/attachments/2024/08/28/jmtr_24_22.pdf) [M, 5 studies]; systematic review concluding positive effects but no pooled effect size: [Monib et al., PMC11774797](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11774797/) [SR]
- Guo et al. (2014), 6.9M sessions on 4 edX courses: video length the strongest predictor of engagement; median engagement time at most 6 minutes regardless of length; 0-3 min videos: 75% of sessions lasted over three-quarters of the video; probability of attempting the following problem fell with length (56%, 48%, 43%, 41%, 31% across five length buckets); talking-head interleaving raised problem attempts (46% vs 33%) in one course; Khan-style tablet-drawn tutorials held attention 1.5-2x as long as slides/code with 40% followed by a problem attempt; videos planned for the online format outperformed chopped-up lectures (55% vs 41% problem attempts, one natural experiment, n=1 pair of courses); students engage differently with lectures vs tutorials (tutorials re-watched/skimmed). [Paper PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf) [Obs, large N, confounded, engagement not learning]
- Noetel et al. (2021), RER 91(2), 204-236: 105 studies, N = 7,776; replacing other methods with video gave a small positive effect on learning (moderate vs static text, small vs live lecturer); a 2024 preprint disputes the original analysis' framing. [AERA summary](https://www.aera.net/tabid/19324/mid/50329/default.aspx); [preprint critique](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202401.0247) [M/SR, Sec on effect labels]
- Attention in video-based learning: a secondary analysis of four experiments (N = 152; 3-6 minute videos, EEG, quizzes) found trait inattention did not predict video-quiz retention. [PMC12538096](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12538096/) [Exp, Sec]

### Inferences
- Treat "short-form" as a spectrum: unit length (60-90 s) is fine; the risk factor is *fragmentation and feed context* (task switching, no narrative continuity, no consolidation time). Each knowledge unit should be a self-contained narrative with a closing recap (as CLT-bLM already has), and consecutive videos in the feed should be sequenced by concept dependency (a series/playlist), not shuffled.
- Because feed environments invite swiping before consolidation, include an end-of-video retrieval prompt and a pause/consolidation beat (see Q4) to counter the faster-forgetting finding. Hypothesis, not tested for this design.
- Evaluate learning outcomes on delayed tests (24-72 h+) and transfer items; immediate quiz gains (like the Reel Deal result) are insufficient to claim superiority.
- Tutorials-with-motion (Khan-style progressive drawing) and talking-head/face interleaving have observational support for engagement; for animation-based units, progressive reveal synchronized with narration is the analog.

### Gaps
- No study found that tests a standard vertical swipe feed with educational content and delayed learning outcomes; none that compares vertical vs horizontal format; none specifically on captions/on-screen text in vertical educational video (captions, redundancy, and split-attention were not retrieved).
- Original text of the Nature papers not accessed (effect sizes, stimuli details); the Galaxus/Spektrum experiment and the "Chinese study" mentioned in a podcast transcript were not traced to originals.
- Edutainment vs learning-focused comparison for TikTok-style content: not found as a controlled study.
- Narrative/storytelling effects on learning (meta-analysis) not searched; no figures recorded.

## Q4. Frameworks with an embedded question/interaction at the end or midway; interactive-video effect sizes

### Takeaway
Interactive video with embedded questions shows moderate pooled gains (Ploetzner 2024: g about 0.52 for enhanced interaction features; older ERIC-listed review 0.53) but heterogeneity is high and many primary studies confound time-on-task. Practice testing in general has a robust, larger effect (g about 0.6-0.7). I found no named "Hook-Teach-Check" or "Predict-Explain-Observe in short video" framework with empirical validation; these appear to be design heuristics.

### Cited Findings
- Ploetzner (2024), Educational Research Review: meta-analysis of instructional videos with enhanced interaction features (prompts/questions vs navigation features) found g = 0.52 (0.522 in another citing source) in favor of interactive video; many studies combined feature types so the driver is unclear, and about half did not control or report learning time. [Springer (Instructional Science 2024) citing paper](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5) [M, Sec via citing papers]
- Another paper cites Ploetzner as evidence interactive features can have limited or even negative effects when misaligned with instructional design principles. [same Springer page](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5) [Sec]
- Older ERIC record: 63 studies of interactive video instruction, mean achievement effect 0.53, heterogeneous. [ERIC ED321761](https://nufind.nu.edu.sa/EdsRecord/eric,ED321761) [M, old, Sec]
- Embedded questions in pre-class videos: some studies show achievement gains, others (e.g., Deng & Gao) no post-test benefit. [Springer record](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10639-023-12303-5) [Sec]
- Interactive media in science education meta-analysis (2025): 25 studies, d about 0.495, I-squared about 89% (very high heterogeneity). [Search record](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s10639-023-12303-5) (attribution of this figure rests on the search summary) [M, Sec]
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017), RER 87(3), 659-701, practice-testing meta-analysis: practice tests beat restudying and all other comparison conditions; secondary sources report g = 0.61 (fixed) or 0.70 (random effects), classroom 0.67, lab 0.62; feedback difference not significant; multiple-choice the best single format (reports differ on number of studies: 118 articles / 272 effects, N = 15,472). [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.3102/0034654316689306); [WSU summary](https://education.wsu.edu/documents/2018/01/rethinking-use-tests.pdf/) [M, Sec on numbers]
- A secondary table cites King-Shepard et al. (2025, Educational Psychology Review) reporting prequestioned content g = 0.66 vs 0.01 for non-prequestioned content. I could not trace this to the original paper. [Frontiers Education result (unverified attribution)](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2019.00005/epub) [Sec, unverified]
- A secondary vendor blog cites active-learning video effects of about g 0.33 retention and 0.43 transfer; low reliability. [Fora Soft blog](https://www.forasoft.com/learn/elearning-video/articles-elearning/what-makes-video-interactive) [Sec, low quality]
- Guo et al. (2014) also show tutorials followed by problems get attempts 31-56% of the time, which indicates learners often skip the interaction when it is not integrated. [Paper PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf) [Obs]
- Brame (2016) recommends tying videos to assignments and building in interactive questions to promote active processing. [PMC5132380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/)

### Inferences
- The current CLT-bLM ends with a recap (receptive); replacing or supplementing it with a one-item retrieval check (tap a multiple-choice answer, then show the answer and one-sentence explanation) is the highest-evidence upgrade candidate (practice testing g about 0.6-0.7, interactive video g about 0.5), subject to the caveat that those figures include time-on-task confounds.
- Level-based variant of the question: L1 = recall/near-transfer MCQ on the worked example; L2 = identify the next step or error; L3 = the "what-if" prediction followed by reveal (Predict-Observe-Explain-like). The expertise-reversal logic supports this; no direct study of this exact mapping was found.
- A prequestion/hook combination (open with a question the video then answers) is theoretically attractive (hook plus pretesting benefit) but the only quantitative support I found is an unverified secondary citation; label as hypothesis.
- Named frameworks. From general background knowledge (NOT sourced in this session; treat as unverified): Hook-Teach-Check and "problem-first micro-lesson" are practitioner heuristics; the "Hook Model" (Nir Eyal) is a product-habit framework for consumer apps, not a learning framework, and should not be cited as evidence of learning gains; Predict-Observe-Explain (White & Gunstone) and productive failure (Kapur) are better-established, though productive-failure results are for longer problem-solving sessions rather than 60-90 s videos. Checking these in a follow-up pass is recommended before they are included in a report.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed validation found for "Hook-Teach-Check", "Hook Model", or "SOLO/Bloom-aligned micro-lesson" as video frameworks.
- Ploetzner (2024) full text (moderators for question type, placement mid vs end, feedback) not accessed; only citing summaries.
- No meta-analysis isolating embedded questions in video was found (search explicitly looked).
- Predict-Explain-Observe in short video: no empirical sources retrieved.
- The question of end-of-video vs mid-video question placement in 60-90 s clips is untested in the sources found.
