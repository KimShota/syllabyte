# Multimedia Learning Theory and Evidence-Based Design for 60-90 s Educational Micro-Videos

Scope note: Many primary papers were paywalled or blocked (Wiley, SAGE, PMC, Cambridge). Numbers below come from abstracts, search snippets, and secondary summaries unless marked "read in full". Where sources disagree, both values are given. Items from my own background knowledge that I could not source are listed under Gaps, not in Cited Findings. No source found studies 60-90 s videos specifically.

## Key Question 1: Which multimedia principles have the strongest, most replicated effect sizes, and which are contested or limited to certain learner levels?

### Takeaway
Mayer's narrative-review medians (d about 0.5-1.7) are consistently larger than independent meta-analyses (g about 0.2-0.5). The best-supported principles by independent meta-analysis are signaling, segmenting, modality (with boundary conditions), worked examples and personalization. The multimedia principle, coherence, redundancy and pre-training rest mostly on Mayer-lab medians with fewer independent meta-analytic confirmations. Expertise reversal is real: scaffolds help novices and hurt experts. Voice and embodiment/instructor-presence effects are the most contested.

### Cited Findings
**Mayer-lab medians (narrative reviews, probably inflated vs. meta-analyses)**
- Mayer (2017, J. Computer Assisted Learning) abstract medians: multimedia d = 1.67 (5 comparisons); coherence 0.70; signaling 0.46; redundancy 0.87; spatial contiguity 0.79; segmenting 0.70; pre-training 0.46; modality 0.72. Some principles are stronger for low- than high-knowledge learners — [MetaTOC listing of Mayer 2017](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning). The abstract values differ from the older handbook values below, which suggests different editions or analyses.
- Older medians (a slide deck summarizing the handbook): signaling 0.52 (6 studies), redundancy 0.72 (5), segmenting 0.98 (3), pre-training 0.85 (5), multimedia 1.02 (17) — [search result deck](https://open.umich.edu/sites/default/files/downloads/um-multimedialearning-final.pdf) (attribution to this exact deck is approximate).
- 2012 Handbook chapters: coherence median d = 1.32, 10 of 11 tests positive; segmenting 0.79, 10 of 10 tests; pre-training 0.92, 7 of 7 tests (earlier 2009 edition: 0.85, 5 of 5) — [Cambridge Handbook segmenting/pre-training/modality chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258); [pre-training chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/pretraining-principle/01791D57F5D4164251269E6DF56A8BF1).
- Mayer reports the multimedia principle (words + pictures > words alone) at median d > 1 across 13 experiments; he says the coherence principle is the one most violated in California textbooks — [search summary of Mayer interview](https://cepsj.si/index.php/cepsj/article/download/1238/595).
- Generative activity principle: median d = 0.71, 37 of 44 tests positive (Mayer commentary table, 2021) — [EdTechBooks, Future of Multimedia Learning](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349).
- Voice principle (human > machine voice): 5 of 6 tests, median d = 0.74 (Mayer, Sobko & Mautone 2003 basis; older studies with old TTS) — [Cambridge: Personalization, Voice and Image Principles](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/personalization-voice-and-image-principles/97F9B31362E6491806A4718FECCADE3D).

**Independent meta-analyses**
- Signaling: Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018, Educational Research Review): retention g = 0.53 [0.42, 0.64] (139 comparisons), transfer g = 0.33 [0.22, 0.43] (k = 70), cognitive load g = 0.25; N = 12,201 — [ACU summary](https://inspire.acu.edu.au/articles/signalling). Alpizar, Adesope & Wong (2020): d = 0.38, 29 studies, 44 effects, N = 2,726. Richter, Scheiter & Eitel (2016): r = 0.17 [.11, .22], moderated by prior knowledge. Prior-knowledge and static-vs-dynamic signaling moderators are inconsistent across meta-analyses — same ACU link, plus [QIC-WD summary](https://qic-wd.org/node/672). The study count for Schneider differs across sources (103 vs. 145).
- Segmenting: Rey et al. (2019, Educational Psychology Review): 56 investigations, 88 pairwise comparisons, small-to-medium effects on retention and transfer. High-prior-knowledge learners benefited more for retention. The effect was not changed by extra processing time, self-pacing, or rewatching — [Rey et al. 2019](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4); [summary](https://is.tuebingen.mpg.de/publications/rey2018segmenting); moderators via [Springer Instructional Science 2024 article summary](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5) (secondary, verify).
- Modality: Ginns (2005, Learning & Instruction) is the standard meta-analysis; Mayer cites a median of d about 1.02 — [Cambridge modality chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/modality-principle/E5CD6E01CEA0B568CE260F66A3CD0D1F). A later meta-analysis of 91 studies found much smaller effects (retention d = 0.24, transfer d = 0.25), concentrated in system-paced presentations, dynamic pictures, and short materials — [Advances in Psychological Science, 2016 listing](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/adps/EN/Y2016/V24/I3/335) (author attribution unverified).
- Worked examples (math): Barbieri et al. (2023, Educational Psychology Review) g = 0.48 (abstract); an earlier poster reports g = 0.43 [0.31, 0.55], 185 effect sizes. Correct examples alone outperformed incorrect or mixed examples; self-explanation prompts moderated the effect in one report (moderator reports conflict between abstract and poster) — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1); [summary](https://learningandthebrain.com/blog/?p=7218).
- Expertise reversal: Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025, Learning and Instruction 98:102142), 176 effects, N = 5,924: high instructional assistance helps low-prior-knowledge learners (d = +0.505) and hurts high-prior-knowledge learners (d = -0.428); heterogeneity I-squared about 88-91%. These numbers come only from a weak secondary web document — [paper DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.learninstruc.2025.102142); [secondary doc](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/npm/opencode-engram-learning@1.11.1/docs/06-visual-encoding.md). Verify against the paper.
- Corpus-level meta-analysis of Mayer's multimedia research (1990-2022), overall g = 0.37. The most reliable gains came from removing seductive details (coherence), modality, personalization, and generative activities (self-explanation). Text + diagrams was strong. Animation, games and simulations were inconsistent (games and simulations g about 0.41). VR showed no significant advantage. Effects were larger for transfer/inference than recall, for STEM/medical than liberal arts, and for university students than for elementary or graduate students. Many effects have shrunk over publication years — [Hendrick Substack summary of ScienceDirect PII S1747938X25000673](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually) (secondary; authors and title not given in the post).
- Instructor presence (face in video): Alemdag (2022) meta-analysis of 20 experiments: no significant effect on learning or social presence. Instructor-present videos increased cognitive load and motivation. Showing the hand only was marginally better for knowledge acquisition — [Alemdag meta-analysis](https://avesis.metu.edu.tr/yayin/8df0f0a3-6154-4f4b-9333-5108f1e4b1f1/effects-of-instructor-present-videos-on-learning-cognitive-load-motivation-and-social-presence-a-meta-analysis). Zhang & Yu (2023, 47 studies): learning improves when gestures, eye guidance and facial expression are integrated with speech — [search summary](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9786415/) (attribution of the 47-study claim is from the search summary). Fiorella & Mayer (2018) list matching instructor gender and showing the instructor's face among ineffective features and segmenting plus multiple perspectives among effective ones — [ECR Hub](https://ecrhub.org/publications/what-works-and-doesnt-work-instructional-video).
- Voice with modern TTS: Craig & Schroeder (2017) found a modern TTS voice gave higher transfer than classic TTS or a human voice. Dincer (2022) found no significant difference between modern TTS and human voice. Another study found human voice gave better persona/engagement ratings but no learning difference — [Voice effect revisited (J. Pedagogical Research 2022)](https://doaj.org/article/a14435a205f54240bdd0b0db2fa06f91); [Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/multimedia-learning/personalization-voice-and-image-principles/97F9B31362E6491806A4718FECCADE3D).

### Inferences
- Plan design decisions around independent-meta-analysis magnitudes (g about 0.25-0.55 for most multimedia principles), not Mayer's medians; each principle is a small-to-medium additive effect.
- For a TTS pipeline, the voice principle's penalty looks largely gone with modern neural TTS, but evidence is thin (a few studies). Use a high-quality neural voice and conversational wording (personalization), and skip the "human voice" worry.
- Because the corpus meta-analysis finds university students and STEM benefit most, the course audience (university CS) is among the best-suited populations. Effects are weaker for animation than for diagrams, so animation should be purposeful (showing process or change), not decorative.
- Expertise reversal means a fixed, heavily scaffolded script suits novices on a topic; learners who already know the prerequisite may be slowed by redundancy. A skip/"I know this" option on pre-training segments is the mitigation.

### Gaps
- Could not read Mayer (2017) Table or the 2021 Cambridge Handbook (3rd ed.) tables; the discrepancies among 0.70/0.46/1.32 coherence-type values are unresolved.
- No independent meta-analysis located for coherence, spatial contiguity, or pre-training (my background knowledge suggests meta-analyses exist for coherence/seductive details and redundancy, e.g., Adesope & Nesbit 2012, but I did not verify any numbers). Redundancy: only the citation was confirmed.
- Split-attention (Ginns 2006) and the imagination effect: no numbers found.
- Exact title/authors of the 1990-2022 corpus meta-analysis not verified.

## Key Question 2: Does the transient information effect argue against long continuous narration in short video, and what does it imply for segment count/pacing?

### Takeaway
Yes, in principle: long, dense spoken explanations lose to equivalent static text, and the modality advantage can reverse when spoken segments are long. Remedies in the literature are shorter verbal chunks, segmenting, and pausing. The evidence base is small, mostly lab studies with short passages, and mixed for animation.

### Cited Findings
- Definition: loss of learning when information disappears before the learner can process or link it to new information (Sweller, Ayres & Kalyuga 2011, p. 220) — [Macquarie University record of Leahy & Sweller work](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/); [Wong et al. context](https://lead.ube.fr/?p=5776).
- Leahy & Sweller (2011, Applied Cognitive Psychology 25, 943-951): with long audio text, the usual modality effect reversed (written text beat spoken text). With shorter text, the expected modality advantage did not appear either. Result depends on text length and element interactivity — [Macquarie record](https://researchers.mq.edu.au/en/publications/cognitive-load-theory-and-the-effects-of-transient-information-on/); [Birmingham record on element interactivity](https://birmingham.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/52472d3c-eb17-42be-8c05-609405d3b309).
- Mitigation: shorter verbal instructions and segmenting key instructions (Singh, Marcus & Ayres 2012) — [Northern Ireland education CLT guide](https://www.education-ni.gov.uk/sites/default/files/2025-09/Cognitive%20Load%20Theory.PDF).
- Animation: transience weakens the dynamic-over-static advantage in an origami/hand-procedure task, but reducing transience did not always help children learning a complex hand procedure, and long-section animation sometimes kept its advantage — [LEAD article](https://lead.ube.fr/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/001246-reducing-the-transience-effect-of-animations-does-not-always-lead-to-better-performance-in-children-learning-a-complex-hand-procedure.pdf); [Springer 11423-021-10076-7](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11423-021-10076-7).
- The 2016 modality meta-analysis found narration advantages concentrated in system-paced, dynamic, short material (d = 0.24-0.25 retention/transfer) — [APS listing](https://journal.psych.ac.cn/adps/EN/Y2016/V24/I3/335).
- Segmenting meta-analysis: small-to-medium benefit, larger for higher-prior-knowledge learners; no extra benefit from self-pacing or rewatching per one secondary summary — [Rey et al. 2019](https://www.doi.org/10.1007/S10648-018-9456-4); [Springer 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5).
- Speaking rate in MOOC videos ranged 48-254 wpm (mean 156, SD 31). Engagement rose with speaking rate (up to 2x); the authors note engagement is not learning — read in full: [Guo, Kim & Rubin 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf).

### Inferences
- A 60-90 s video at about 150 wpm is roughly 150-225 spoken words. That is short in total but may still be one continuous transient stream, so the risk is density per second, not duration.
- Practical implication: break narration into 3-5 short bursts (about 10-20 s of speech each, 1-2 new ideas) separated by 1-2 s of silence in which the visual stays static or builds, so the learner can integrate. A pause button counts as the learner-paced form of segmenting.
- Key diagrams should persist on screen while the corresponding narration plays and afterward (so information is not only transient). Avoid animating and narrating a multi-step derivation in one pass with no persistent trace.
- Do not assume the 60-90 s limit makes segmentation unnecessary: nothing found shows that short total length removes the transient-information problem; the effect scales with element interactivity.

### Gaps
- No study of transience in videos under 2 minutes with university-level abstract content.
- Optimal burst length and pause duration in seconds: not found; the numbers above are design heuristics, not evidence.

## Key Question 3: Optimal video length, benefits and limits of segmenting and learner control for a 60-90 s clip

### Takeaway
Guo et al. show engagement (watch time) peaks for videos under about 3 minutes and median watch time never exceeds 6 minutes; Brame recommends short chunks. But engagement is not learning, and no rigorous study isolates length as a causal moderator of learning. A 60-90 s clip is inside the engagement-optimal range. The learning gain depends on design and on pairing with activities, not on length itself.

### Cited Findings
- Guo, Kim & Rubin (2014, ACM Learning@Scale), 6.9 million watching sessions, 862 videos, 127,839 students, four edX courses (all math/science, Fall 2012): median engagement at most 6 minutes regardless of video length; students often did not reach halfway on videos over 9 minutes; the shortest videos (0-3 min) had the highest engagement, with 75% of sessions lasting over three quarters of the video; the share of watch sessions followed by a problem attempt fell from 56% to 31% across five length buckets (shortest to longest) — read in full: [Guo et al. 2014 PDF](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf).
- Same paper: engagement is a proxy "necessary but not sufficient" for learning; Khan-style tablet drawing tutorials held attention 1.5x-2x longer than slides or code; informal talking-head insertions before problems associated with higher problem attempt (46% vs. 33% in one course); faster speaking (up to 2x engagement); pre-production planning correlated with higher engagement. Authors recommend segmenting lessons into chunks under 6 minutes — same PDF.
- Brame (2016, CBE Life Sciences Education 15(4):es6) frames video design as managing cognitive load (signaling, segmenting, weeding, matching modality), maximizing engagement (conversational style, appropriate length — summaries cite 6-9 min as the upper practical bound) and promoting active learning via guiding questions and embedded tasks — [Brame via UBC summary](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=568); [PMC5132380](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5132380/) (not retrieved in full).
- Noetel et al. (2021, Review of Educational Research 91(2):204-236): 105 studies (RCT-based), N = 7,776 students. Video replacing existing teaching: g = 0.28 (small positive); video added to existing teaching: g = 0.80. Videos were most effective in interactive environments, with suggestions to embed questions (H5P/EdPuzzle). Videos did not beat more interactive live lessons but did beat equally or less interactive ones — [AERA release](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Video-Improves-Learning-in-Higher-Education-A-Systematic-Review); [Behavioral Scientist](https://behavioralscientist.org/should-video-lectures-be-the-new-normal-in-higher-ed/). Critique: a 2024 preprint argues the review ignores participant demographics and context and overstates conclusions — [Preprints.org](https://www.preprints.org/manuscript/202401.0247).
- Ploetzner (2024) meta-analysis: videos with enhanced interaction features g = 0.52. Many included studies combined enhanced and navigation features, and about half did not control learning time. A follow-up experiment (N = 128) controlling learning time found no effect of navigation features or self-explanation prompts; a related study found self-explanation prompts raised intrinsic and extraneous load without learning gain — [Beege & Ploetzner, Instructional Science 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5) (secondary summary).
- Microlearning evidence: search found no rigorous meta-analysis isolating video duration. Duration convention (2-7 min) is definitional; a vendor blog claims video length itself shows no significant effect on outcomes (low reliability); a 2024 vocational study found repeated short-video access improved delayed practical performance — [UNNES proceedings meta-analysis](https://proceedings.unnes.ac.id/veic/article/view/2849) (very large effect sizes, only 25 articles, treat as unreliable); [JPV study](https://journal.uny.ac.id/index.php/jpv/article/view/95712).

### Inferences
- The "6-minute rule" is an engagement observation from MOOC logs, not a learning threshold. It supports "shorter is better for completion" but does not say 60-90 s outperforms 5 minutes for retention.
- For learning, the likely binding constraints in a 60-90 s unit are density and activity, not length: Noetel and Ploetzner suggest the largest gains come when video is paired with interactivity/practice, and the video is a supplement rather than a replacement. A micro-video should therefore end in a retrieval/application hook rather than act as a standalone.
- Learner control of a 60-90 s clip (pause, replay, 0.75x-1.5x speed): the evidence (Rey) shows self-pacing is not a necessary component of the segmenting effect, so built-in pauses/gaps can substitute; providing the player controls is still cheap.

### Gaps
- No study comparing 60-90 s vs. 3-6 min videos on retention/transfer for equal content.
- Could not read Noetel's moderator tables (video length, interactivity, subject, quiz presence) — numbers other than g = 0.28 and 0.80 are not available.
- Guo et al. covered math/science MOOCs only, with no learning outcomes; their own text states this.

## Key Question 4: Which video-design elements (signaling, pre-training, generative activities, on-screen text vs. narration) most improve retention and transfer?

### Takeaway
Best-supported elements for a micro-video: signaling (g about 0.4-0.5 retention, about 0.3 transfer), coherence (removing seductive details), modality (narration with diagrams rather than dense on-screen text), personalization, pre-training for novices, worked examples (g about 0.45-0.5), and generative/retrieval activities (median d about 0.7 in Mayer's tally, but prompts embedded in video had null or negative effects in controlled experiments).

### Cited Findings
- Signaling: see Key Question 1 (Schneider 2018; Alpizar 2020; Richter 2016).
- Pre-training (teach names and characteristics of key concepts before the lesson): median d = 0.92 (7/7 tests; 2012) or 0.85 (5/5; 2009) or 0.46 (2017 abstract); benefits strongest for low-prior-knowledge learners — [Cambridge pre-training chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/multimedia-learning/pretraining-principle/01791D57F5D4164251269E6DF56A8BF1); [Mayer 2017 abstract](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning).
- Modality / on-screen text: learn more from pictures + narration than pictures + printed words (d about 1.0 per Mayer; 0.24-0.25 in a later 91-study meta-analysis), with effects concentrated in short, system-paced material; redundancy (on-screen text duplicating narration) hurts, Mayer median 0.72-0.87 — see sources in Key Question 1.
- Coherence/weeding: remove music, busy backgrounds, extraneous detail (Brame guideline); the corpus meta-analysis shows seductive-detail removal among the most reliable gains — [Brame via UBC](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=568); [Hendrick summary](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually).
- Generative learning: median d = 0.71 (generative activity principle); self-explanation among the most reliable per the corpus meta-analysis; the eight strategies are summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting — [EdTechBooks](https://edtechbooks.org/docx/10349); [Fiorella & Mayer 2016 DOI](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-015-9348-9) (per-strategy effect sizes not retrieved).
- Embedded prompts in video: Ploetzner overall interaction g = 0.52, but confounded; controlled N = 128 experiment found no effects of navigation or self-explanation prompts; another study found prompts increased cognitive load without benefit — [Instructional Science 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11251-024-09693-5).
- Worked examples: g = 0.48 for math (see Key Question 1). Correct examples alone beat mixed. Self-explanation prompts' moderating role is reported inconsistently — [Barbieri et al.](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-023-09745-1).
- Instructor face: no learning benefit on average (Alemdag 2022), more cognitive load; hand/pointing and gesture integrated with speech are beneficial — see Key Question 1.
- Personalization (conversational "you" style) and conversational tone are among the most reliable effects in the corpus meta-analysis; Guo et al. found informal/personal-feeling production associated with higher engagement — [Hendrick summary](https://carlhendrick.substack.com/p/which-multimedia-strategies-actually); [Guo et al. 2014](https://www.cs.rochester.edu/hci/pubs/pdfs/edX-MOOC-video-production-and-engagement_LAS-2014.pdf).

### Inferences
- Hook design: Mayer-style coherence says a decorative or anecdotal hook (seductive detail) costs learning; a hook that is a content-relevant question or prediction is both coherent and a generative prompt. Make the hook the first retrieval/prediction task, not entertainment.
- Pre-training fits better as a 5-10 s "terms and prerequisite anchor" than as a separate segment; for a course with sequenced knowledge units, a one-line recap of the prerequisite unit is the pre-training analog.
- Signaling (highlight, color-match narrated term to visual, arrows, progressive reveal) is nearly free in Manim and has the most consistent independent meta-analytic support; use it every beat.
- On-screen text: keep to labels/keywords/equations; do not caption the narration verbatim (redundancy). Captions for accessibility are a separate consideration; no evidence on this was gathered.
- Generative activity must be low-load: a "pause and predict the next step" or one self-explain question at the end is supported by the general generative-learning literature, but the in-video prompt evidence is weak. Treat as a hypothesis to A/B test.

### Gaps
- No per-strategy effect sizes for Fiorella & Mayer (2016).
- No data on prediction/pretest questions or retrieval practice inside <2 min videos.
- No evidence retrieved on captions vs. no captions, or on Manim-style animation vs. static diagrams for CS concepts specifically (corpus meta-analysis says animation is inconsistent).

## Key Question 5: Do these imply a better overall structure than Prepare/Initiate/Deliver/End?

### Takeaway
Partly. The evidence does not contradict a 5-part flow, but it argues for re-weighting it: replace the engagement-style hook with a content-relevant prediction and pre-training anchor, segment "Deliver" into short signaled bursts with pauses, make the worked example a faded/predict-then-reveal one, and end with a retrieval or transfer prompt instead of a passive recap. These are inferences from the principle-level evidence above; no study compares overall micro-video structures.

### Cited Findings
- Brame's three-part frame (manage cognitive load, maximize engagement, promote active learning) is the closest published video-specific structure; it prescribes guiding questions and tasks around the video — [Brame summary](https://blogs.ubc.ca/videolearninghub/?p=568).
- Noetel: largest gains when video supplements instruction and sits in an interactive environment (g = 0.80 vs. 0.28) — [AERA](https://www.aera.net/Newsroom/Video-Improves-Learning-in-Higher-Education-A-Systematic-Review).
- Mayer's grouping: principles to reduce extraneous processing (coherence, signaling, redundancy, contiguity), manage essential processing (segmenting, pre-training, modality), and foster generative processing (personalization, voice, embodiment, generative activities) — [Mayer 2017 abstract](https://www.metatoc.com/papers/105408-using-multimedia-for-e-learning); [Cambridge Handbook chapter](https://cambridge.org/core/books/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-for-managing-essential-processing-in-multimedia-learning-segmenting-pretraining-and-modality-principles/DD24C2F48B9B1277CE59F78276110258).
- Expertise reversal: help novices with more guidance, reduce for experts (d = +0.505 vs. -0.428 per a secondary report) — see Key Question 1.

### Inferences
**Candidate structure: "Anchor - Predict - Chunk - Example - Retrieve" (about 75 s; word budget about 160-200 words at about 150 wpm)**
1. Anchor and predict (about 8-10 s). One-sentence prerequisite or term pre-training (pre-training; cue a skip for experts), then a content-relevant question the learner can commit to ("What happens if ...?"). Replaces the generic hook and the "objective" statement; the question is the objective.
2. Core idea in 2 chunks (about 12-18 s each). One idea per chunk, visual builds with signaling, narration in short bursts, persistent diagram, 1-2 s silent gap between chunks (segmenting + transient-information mitigation). No decorative animation, no verbatim on-screen text, conversational voice.
3. Worked example (about 15-20 s). One concrete instance (e.g., a small dataset/network). Optionally fade the last step: freeze and ask "what's the next step?" for 2-3 s, then reveal (worked-example effect; generative prediction; expertise-reversal-aware fading).
4. Retrieve and transfer (about 8-12 s). Return to the opening question and answer it; one-sentence recap; end with one far/near-transfer question for the learner to try or to be asked in the next unit (testing/generative principle).
Mapping to CLT-bLM: Prepare -> Anchor/Predict; Initiate -> folded into the question; Deliver x2 -> Core (2 chunks) + Worked example; End -> Retrieve/transfer. Same number of beats but different content rules, so it is a re-weighting, not a new skeleton.
- Because effect sizes are individually small-to-medium, the most defensible comparison is an A/B test of the original script vs. a revised script varying one or two features at a time (signaling + persistent diagram; predict-then-reveal; retrieval ending), measured on delayed retention and transfer.
- For variable audiences (ML vs. Computational Social Science), the pre-training line and fading step should adapt to prerequisite knowledge (expertise reversal).

### Gaps
- No empirical test of the proposed structure; it is a theory-derived hypothesis.
- Nothing found comparing overall structures for videos under 2 minutes (e.g., problem-first vs. explanation-first). Related literature (productive failure, prequestion effects, retrieval practice, spacing) was not searched here and could support or alter steps 1 and 4.
- Unsourced background knowledge not included above because I could not verify it in this session: worked-example fading and the imagination effect (Cooper et al., 2001; Leahy & Sweller 2005 — checked via no source), the 2019-2021 prequestion/interpolated-testing-in-video literature (e.g., Szpunar et al., 2013), and meta-analytic estimates for the split-attention effect (Ginns 2006) and redundancy (Adesope & Nesbit 2012). These should be looked up before use.
