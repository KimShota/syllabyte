# Learning-science techniques that could beat "hook, objective, explain, example, recap" in a 60-90 s micro-video

Verification key. Items marked [PRIMARY] were read in the paper's full text or PDF in this session. Items marked [SNIPPET] come from search-result summaries or secondary pages and should be re-checked against the paper before being quoted. Several secondary sources conflicted on numbers, and those conflicts are flagged. Claims I know from background knowledge but could not source in this session are listed under Gaps, not under Cited Findings.

## 0. Evidence by technique (effect sizes, boundary conditions, fit for a 60-90 s video)

### Takeaway
Retrieval practice (g about 0.5 in classrooms), self-explanation prompts (g = 0.55), worked examples (g = 0.48 in maths), case comparison (d about 0.5-0.6, sources conflict) and signaling (g about 0.3-0.5) have moderate, meta-analytically supported effects. Prequestions (g = 0.54) help only the specifically asked content, and general learning is about 0 (g = 0.04). Productive failure and curiosity hooks have the weakest and most conditional support for a 60-90 s format. Almost every strong technique needs a learner response (a pause, a prediction, or a follow-up quiz) and not more narration.

### Cited Findings

**Retrieval practice / testing effect**
- Rowland (2014, Psychological Bulletin): 159 effect sizes from 61 studies; testing beat restudy at g = 0.50 [SNIPPET for the totals, PRIMARY for the abstract]. In the full text, the initial-test-type moderator gave cued recall g = 0.61, free recall g = 0.29 and recognition g = 0.29, with the authors warning that cued recall was confounded with feedback use (44% vs 11% of effect sizes). Initial recall tests yielded larger benefits than recognition tests. — [Rowland 2014 PDF](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Rowland (2014) feedback moderator: testing followed by feedback g = 0.73 vs without feedback g = 0.39 (as quoted by Yang et al.). Adesope et al. (2017) found no feedback moderation (with feedback g = 0.63; without g = 0.60). So the feedback evidence is inconsistent across meta-analyses. — [Yang et al. 2021 PDF](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Adesope, Trevisan & Sundararajan (2017, Review of Educational Research, "Rethinking the Use of Tests"): practice tests beat restudy and all other comparison conditions; 272 independent effect sizes (the article count varies between sources, 118 vs 188); g about 0.61 (random-effects 0.70 is reported by one blog only and could not be verified). Largest effects at a 1-6 day lag between practice test and final test. Benefits were larger when practice and final test formats matched. Classroom g about 0.67 vs lab g about 0.62 [SNIPPET]. — [WSU record](https://rex.libraries.wsu.edu/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Rethinking-the-Use-of-Tests-A/99900601154801842); [Frontiers review citing the 0.50-0.61 range](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2018.02412/pdf); [summary page](https://www.tieonline.com/article/2232/the-benefits-of-practice-testing)
- Yang, Luo, Vadillo, Yu & Shanks (2021, Psychological Bulletin): 222 studies, 48,478 students, classroom quizzing g = 0.499 overall (about 0.43 after publication-bias correction per a secondary source). Versus no activity/filler g = 0.610; versus restudy g = 0.330; versus other elaborative strategies (e.g., note-taking, summarizing) g = 0.095, not significant. [PRIMARY for the abstract and moderator numbers below; secondary for the control-condition split]
  - Corrective feedback: g = 0.537 with vs 0.374 without.
  - Transfer to untested material: g = 0.321; to rephrased questions: g = 0.558.
  - Moderators include test-format consistency, material matching, number of repetitions, test timing, and control condition.
  - The review reports gains on factual knowledge, concept comprehension and knowledge application.
  — [Yang et al. 2021 PDF](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf); [HKBU record](https://scholars.hkbu.edu.hk/en/publications/testing-quizzing-boosts-classroom-learning-a-systematic-and-meta-/)
- Rowland (2014) delay moderator: g = 0.69 for final tests given at least 1 day later vs g = 0.41 same day [SNIPPET, from a blog]. — [Mindomax summary](https://www.mindomax.com/the-testing-effect)
- Video-lecture specifics: Szpunar, Khan & Schacter (2013, PNAS) found interpolated tests during a 21-minute statistics video lecture reduced mind-wandering and improved retention versus restudy. Jing, Szpunar & Schacter (2016) with a 40-minute lecture found mind-wandering did not differ between tested and restudy groups. A 2022 paper notes both studies allowed note-taking (a possible confound) and that later results are mixed. [SNIPPET] — [search summary of Szpunar 2013 / Jing 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/); [2022 follow-up](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8964911/)

**Pretesting / prequestioning**
- St. Hilaire, Chan & Ahn (2024, Psychonomic Bulletin & Review), preregistered meta-analysis: specific effect (on the exact prequestioned content) g = 0.54 (k = 97); general effect (on related but non-prequestioned content) g = 0.04 (k = 91). [PRIMARY] — [PDF](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Moderators in the same paper [PRIMARY]:
  - Having learners guess the answer: g = 0.65 vs 0.22 without a guess (the strongest moderator).
  - Factual prequestions (g = 0.58) were marginally larger than conceptual ones (g = 0.28, p = .073).
  - Lower prior knowledge was associated with a larger specific effect (beta = -0.49, p = .094, marginal).
  - Adults g = 0.62 vs grade-school children g = 0.22, p = .020, with few child studies, so it is not robust.
  - The general effect was slightly positive for audiovisual material (g = 0.17) and about zero for text.
  - Pre-2006 general effect g = -0.09; post-2006 g = 0.15. The authors suggest this may reflect designs changing to favour a general effect, not a real shift.
  - Almost no studies manipulated a delay between prequestion and study, and few studies were done in classrooms or MOOCs.
  - The effect depends on the learner finding and attending to the answer: if the answer was not noted during study, performance was at control level (St. Hilaire & Carpenter 2020, described in the meta-analysis).
- Carpenter & Toftness (2017) studied prequestions with video presentations (cited in the meta-analysis). The effect size was not extracted in this session.

**Prediction before reveal (POE: predict-observe-explain)**
- No meta-analysis located in this session. The closest quantified support is the guessing moderator above (guessing g = 0.65 vs 0.22). A "guess then reveal" micro-pause is therefore a practical way to implement POE.

**Productive failure (PF) / problem solving before instruction (PS-I)**
- Sinha & Kapur (2021, Review of Educational Research 91(5), doi 10.3102/00346543211019105). Reported effect sizes differ by source and could not be read from the paper (publisher returned 403):
  - A mirror of the paper reports a publication-bias-corrected Hedges' g = 0.87 favouring PS-I [SNIPPET, unofficial mirror].
  - Kapur's own description: 53 studies, 166 comparisons [SNIPPET, promotional]. An ETH record gives a different count (118 comparisons, 33 articles).
  - A related EdArXiv working paper by the same authors reports corrected g = 0.55 (vs instruction plus problem solving) and g = 0.64 (vs direct instruction), with no difference from scaffolding (g = -0.08) [SNIPPET].
  - Guidance strategies that help students notice their knowledge gaps were associated with larger effects [SNIPPET].
  — [ETH record](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/entities/publication/93a6fe24-dde9-4f59-ad42-fc4476064bdf); [mirror](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105); [Edutopia interview](https://edutopia.org/article/if-youre-not-failing-youre-not-learning)
- Sinha reports PS-I advantages are smaller for procedural content and larger for harder concepts [SNIPPET, news/thesis]. — [TechLearning](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)
- Loibl, Roll & Rummel (2017, Educational Psychology Review 29(4)): review of 21 studies; proposes prior-knowledge activation and awareness of knowledge gaps as the mechanisms; PF "usually shows no special effect" for procedural knowledge and results on transfer are inconsistent [SNIPPET]. — [Springer, Mazziotti et al. 2019 discussion](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6435728/)
- Boundary-condition and replication problems: Mazziotti, Rummel, Deiglmayr & Loibl (2019) found mixed results for younger students; a conceptual replication in Bayesian reasoning found no main effect of PS-I, contrary to the original Loibl & Rummel (2014) [SNIPPET]. — [Mazziotti 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6435728/); [Bayesian replication](https://phfr.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/index/index/docId/3543)
- Practicality: PF's own designs use 10s of minutes of generation and collaboration. Nothing in the sources suggests a 60-90 s version has been tested.

**Worked examples / faded examples**
- Barbieri, Miller-Cotto, Clerjuste & Chawla (2023, Educational Psychology Review): 43 articles, 55 studies, 181 effect sizes, mathematics; mean g = 0.48. Correct examples alone beat incorrect-only or mixed; adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples moderated the effect negatively relative to worked examples without prompts (as reported in the abstract). [SNIPPET] — [Kent State HCRI](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance)
- A faded-examples meta-analysis, Sweller/Renkl/Atkinson primary studies: not retrieved. See Gaps.

**Self-explanation prompts**
- Bisra, Liu, Nesbit, Salimi & Winne (2018, Educational Psychology Review 30:703-725; note: not Review of Educational Research): 69 effect sizes from 64 reports; overall g = 0.55; 20 moderators examined (task, subject, education level, prompt type, duration). Authors conclude prompts help "across a range of instructional conditions". [PRIMARY for the abstract] — [PDF](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- Prompt-type breakdown (interrogative g = 0.56, imperative g = 0.40, pre-directed g = 0.70) and no-extra-explanation g = 0.67 come from a secondary table summary [SNIPPET]. — [Clearing House summary](https://www.clearinghouse.edu.tum.de/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/CHU-KR-25_ENG_Bisra_2018_Selbsterklaerungen.pdf)
- Most included studies used undergraduates [SNIPPET].

**Generative learning activities (Fiorella & Mayer)**
- Fiorella & Mayer (2016, Educational Psychology Review 28(4):717-741), "Eight ways to promote generative learning": summarizing, mapping, drawing, imagining, self-testing, self-explaining, teaching, enacting. It is a narrative review, not a meta-analysis. [SNIPPET] — [Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/cambridge-handbook-of-multimedia-learning/principles-based-on-generative-activity-in-multimedia-learning/EDDABAEDA03EC46788605671F6F75ACC)
- Outlining while reading: g+ = 0.59 on memory tests (Ponce, Mayer & Mendez 2023) [SNIPPET].

**Interleaving and spacing**
- Brunmair & Richter (2019, Psychological Bulletin): 59 studies, 238 effect sizes; overall interleaving g = 0.42; mathematics g = 0.34; paintings g = 0.67; word-based material favoured blocking (g = -0.39). Larger effects when between-category similarity was high and within-category similarity was low. [SNIPPET] — [Preprint PDF](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Cepeda et al. (2006, Psychological Bulletin) meta-analysis found a spacing benefit; counts differ across summaries (254 studies/14,000+ participants vs 839 assessments/317 experiments). Cepeda et al. (2008): the optimal gap grows with the test delay but shrinks as a proportion of it (for example about 20-40% of a one-week delay; another summary says 43% of 7 days to 8% of 350 days). [SNIPPET, figures conflict] — [Cepeda 2008 PDF](https://www.evullab.org/pdf/CepedaVulRohrerWixtedPashler-PS-2008.pdf)
- Dunlosky et al. (2013, Psychological Science in the Public Interest): high utility = practice testing and distributed practice; moderate = elaborative interrogation, self-explanation, interleaved practice; low = summarization, highlighting, keyword mnemonic, imagery for text, rereading. [SNIPPET but widely confirmed] — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)

**Contrasting cases / analogical encoding / case comparison**
- Alfieri, Nokes-Malach & Schunn (2013, Educational Psychologist 48(2):87-113): 57 experiments, 336 tests; case comparison beats traditional methods with a moderate effect. Sources conflict on size (d = .50 vs 0.60). Highlighting similarities helped more than attending to both similarities and differences. [SNIPPET] — [Pitt PDF](https://www.lrdc.pitt.edu/Schunn/papers/ContrastingCasesMeta-AlfieriEtAl2013.pdf); [Scinapse](https://www.scinapse.io/papers/2124645092)

**Concreteness fading**
- Fyfe, McNeil, Son & Goldstone (2014, Educational Psychology Review 26:9-25) is a systematic review, with no pooled effect size. Fyfe, McNeil & Borjas (2015, Learning and Instruction 35:104-120): grade 2-3 students solved more transfer problems after concreteness fading than after concrete-only or abstract-only instruction; concrete-only learners made fewer early errors but transferred worst. Reported benefits held for low- and high-knowledge students (from blog summaries). McNeil & Fyfe (2012) found the fading condition produced the best transfer in undergraduates. [SNIPPET; no effect sizes extracted] — [Fyfe et al. 2014 summary](https://pc.cogs.indiana.edu/?p=885); [Learning Scientists summary](https://www.learningscientists.org/blog/2018/2/1-1); [McNeil & Fyfe 2012](https://pcl.sitehost.iu.edu/rgoldsto/courses/cogscilearning/McNeilFyfe12.pdf)

**Signaling and feedback**
- Schneider, Beege, Nebel & Rey (2018) signaling meta-analysis, as reproduced on a teaching slide: memorization 0.53 (139 studies), transfer 0.33 (70), cognitive load 0.25, motivation 0.13, time on task -0.30. Only a secondary slide was found, so verify the paper before quoting. [SNIPPET] — [Slide deck](https://inspe-sciedu.gricad-pages.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/slides/UE-24-option-rech-5.pdf)
- Wisniewski, Zierer & Hattie (2020, Frontiers in Psychology 10:3087): 435 studies, k = 994, N > 61,000; feedback d = 0.48 on average, with significant heterogeneity; information content of feedback is the key moderator; larger effects on cognitive/motor outcomes than motivation. [SNIPPET, abstract-level] — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2019.03087/full)
- Elaborated feedback beat correctness-only feedback (EF about 0.49, Van der Kleij et al. 2015, item-based feedback) [SNIPPET, reported via a 2021 Frontiers in Education article]. — [Frontiers in Education](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2021.645758/pdf)

**Learning by teaching / protege / Feynman-style framing**
- Kobayashi (2019, Japanese Psychological Research 61(3):192-203): 28 studies; preparing-to-teach alone g = 0.35; teaching after preparing g = 0.56; both vs studying without a teaching expectancy; larger gains when teaching was interactive. [SNIPPET, from a listing] — [MetaTOC](https://metatoc.com/papers/112234-learning-by-preparing-to-teach-and-teaching-a-meta-analysis)
- A 2023 opinion piece argues that Ribosa & Duran's (2022) meta-analysis of creating teaching materials (23 studies, 62 comparisons) blends conceptually different comparisons [SNIPPET]. — [Frontiers](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2023.1095285/full)
- I found no evidence that a "Feynman technique" framing, as a named script device, outperforms self-explanation or retrieval in controlled studies. The supported mechanism is the teaching-expectancy and explaining/retrieval content, not the label.

### Inferences
- In a 60-90 s linear video the practical levers are: (1) a short "pause and answer" (retrieval or prediction) with a feedback reveal, (2) a prequestion with a forced guess targeting the one fact or concept the video will teach, (3) one self-explanation prompt attached to the worked example, (4) signaling and elaborated feedback, and (5) a concreteness-fading ordering inside the example. Spacing, interleaving, and delayed retrieval cannot happen inside one video and must live in the follow-up check and the scheduler.
- Techniques whose effects were measured with minutes-to-hours of learner activity (PF, teaching, generative drawing) are poor fits for the video itself but are candidates for the quick check or a follow-up task.
- The Dunlosky ratings and the meta-analytic gaps agree in direction: retrieval and spacing at the top, self-explanation and interleaving mid, rereading/summarizing at the bottom. A recap that re-states content is closest to the "rereading/restudy" control (Yang 2021: quizzing g = 0.33 vs restudy), which is why replacing it is a high-leverage change.

### Gaps
- No faded-worked-example meta-analysis (Atkinson, Renkl) was retrieved; the worked-example effect (Sweller) is covered here only through Barbieri et al. 2023 for maths. Source needed: Renkl & Atkinson reviews; Crissman 2006 dissertation; Hattie syntheses.
- Roediger & Karpicke (2006, Psychological Science) original numbers (from memory, unverified: after one week, studied-once-then-tested conditions retained clearly more than repeated study) were not retrieved.
- Direct effect sizes for POE, concreteness fading, and Fiorella & Mayer's eight strategies as a meta-analysis were not found.
- Schneider 2018 and Adesope 2017 exact numbers rely on secondary summaries. Cepeda 2006 counts conflict across sources.
- The Sinha & Kapur 2021 abstract could not be read (403). Conflicting counts and effect sizes across mirrors (g = 0.87 vs 0.55/0.64; 53/166 vs 33/118) need verification from the journal page.

## 1. Which techniques have the largest, most robust effects on delayed retention and transfer, and which are practical in about 60-90 s of video?

### Takeaway
By meta-analytic g for learning outcomes, the best-supported options are retrieval with feedback (about 0.5-0.7), self-explanation (0.55), prequestions with guessing (0.65 specific, but about 0 general), worked examples (0.48, maths), case comparison (about 0.5-0.6) and interleaving (0.42; context dependent). Retrieval and prequestioning fit best inside a short video because they need only a pause and a reveal. Transfer effects are generally smaller than retention effects: quizzing transfers to untested material at g = 0.32 and to rephrased questions at g = 0.56, signaling transfer is about 0.33, and prequestions show a general effect of about 0.04.

### Cited Findings
- Practice testing and distributed practice are the only two techniques rated "high utility" by Dunlosky et al. (2013); self-explanation and interleaving were rated moderate. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- Classroom quizzing g = 0.499; vs restudy g = 0.330; vs filler g = 0.610; vs other elaborative strategies g = 0.095 (n.s.). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Larger testing effects at delays of 1+ days than same-day (g = 0.69 vs 0.41, Rowland 2014 via blog), and largest at 1-6 day lags (Adesope 2017). — [Mindomax](https://www.mindomax.com/the-testing-effect); [TIE summary](https://www.tieonline.com/article/2232/the-benefits-of-practice-testing)
- Prequestion specific effect g = 0.54, general effect g = 0.04. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Self-explanation prompts g = 0.55 across task types. — [Bisra et al. 2018](https://www.Gwern.net/doc/psychology/spaced-repetition/2018-bisra.pdf)
- Signaling transfer about 0.33 vs memorization 0.53 (secondary slide). — [Slide deck](https://inspe-sciedu.gricad-pages.univ-grenoble-alpes.fr/slides/UE-24-option-rech-5.pdf)
- Interleaving has a mixed profile (maths g = 0.34, paintings 0.67, verbal material negative). — [Brunmair & Richter 2019](https://www.psychologie.uni-wuerzburg.de/fileadmin/06020400/2019/Brunmair_Richter_in_press__2019_META-ANALYSIS_OF_INTERLEAVED_LEARNING.pdf)
- Embedded-test results in video lectures are mixed across studies. — [2022 study](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8964911/)

### Inferences
- Ranking by (evidence strength x fit to 60-90 s): 1) retrieval/prediction with feedback at the end; 2) a guess-based prequestion at the start for the single target idea; 3) a self-explanation prompt during the worked example; 4) signaling plus elaborated feedback in the reveal; 5) concreteness-fading example order; 6) contrasting two cases when the concept has a confusable neighbour; 7) spacing and interleaving through the quiz scheduler, not the video.
- Because delayed effects are larger than same-day effects for testing, retention should be measured at 7+ days, and a same-session quiz after the video understates the benefit.
- Prequestion benefits are targeted, so the video's prequestion should name the exact concept the quiz and the delayed test will probe.

### Gaps
- Few meta-analyses report delayed transfer separately for short instructional videos; the field mostly measures within-session retention.
- No direct tests of any of these techniques inside a 60-90 s narrated animation were found.

## 2. Problem-first (productive failure / prequestion) vs explanation-first for novices vs advanced learners; expertise reversal

### Takeaway
Evidence for problem-first designs is positive on average for conceptual understanding and transfer, but is strongest with long, collaborative, scaffolded activities and mixed or negative with young learners and procedural content. The prequestion evidence suggests larger benefits for lower prior knowledge on the specific content. Direct expertise-reversal results for these designs are not quantified here: the 2025 meta-analysis of expertise reversal exists but its results were not retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- PS-I (productive failure) meta-analysis (Sinha & Kapur 2021) reports an advantage for PS-I over direct instruction; sources disagree on size (g = 0.87 bias-corrected in one mirror; g = 0.55-0.64 in a related working paper); guidance on noticing gaps is associated with larger effects. — [ETH record](https://www.research-collection.ethz.ch/entities/publication/93a6fe24-dde9-4f59-ad42-fc4476064bdf); [mirror](https://https-sage-cnpereading-com-443.webvpn1.xju.edu.cn/doi/10.3102/00346543211019105)
- PF generally shows no special effect on procedural knowledge and inconsistent transfer (Loibl et al. 2017 as cited by others); mixed results in younger students (Mazziotti et al. 2019); a Bayesian-reasoning replication found no main effect. — [Mazziotti 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6435728/); [replication](https://phfr.bsz-bw.de/frontdoor/index/index/docId/3543)
- Sinha reports procedural content (e.g., standard deviation computation) shows instruction-first about equal to problem-first. — [TechLearning](https://techlearning.com/news/study-productive-failure-a-success-in-education)
- Prequestions: effect marginally larger at lower prior knowledge (beta = -0.49, p = .094); factual prequestions beat conceptual ones (g = 0.58 vs 0.28); no general benefit for non-prequestioned material. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- Expertise reversal definition (Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler & Sweller 2003, Educational Psychologist 38(1):23-31): techniques effective with inexperienced learners can lose effectiveness or turn harmful with experienced learners. A meta-analysis exists: Tetzlaff, Simonsmeier, Peters & Brod (2025, Learning and Instruction 98:102142), but results were not retrieved. Chen, Kalyuga & Sweller (2017) is a review arguing that expertise reversal is a variant of the element interactivity effect. — [Wollongong record](https://ro.uow.edu.au/era/2668); [Springer, Chen et al. 2017 listing](https://link.springer.com/doi/10.1007/s10648-019-09462-8)
- Barbieri et al. 2023: adding self-explanation prompts to worked examples had a negative moderating effect vs examples without prompts, which is the opposite of what Bisra et al. suggest for prompts in general (could reflect learner expertise or time cost; not established). — [Kent State HCRI](https://oaks.kent.edu/hcri/meta-analysis-worked-examples-effect-mathematics-performance)

### Inferences
- A 60-90 s video cannot reproduce a PF activity, so "problem-first" reduces to a 5-10 s guess/prediction (prequestion or POE) before the explanation. This is supported (g about 0.5-0.65 for the targeted content), unlike a full PF design.
- For novice or Level 1 learners: explanation-first with a worked example, concreteness fading, and signaling; the prediction should be low-stakes and factual (prequestion evidence favours factual items) and followed immediately by the answer.
- For Level 3 / more advanced learners: shorter worked example (or a faded example with the final step left to the learner), a conceptual prediction or a "spot the difference" contrast, and a retrieval prompt without scaffolding. This follows the expertise-reversal logic, but the specific mapping to levels is an inference, not a tested result.
- Procedural CS content (tracing code, applying an algorithm) should lean explanation-first with fading; conceptual content (why a data structure behaves a certain way) is where problem-first has the best chance.

### Gaps
- Tetzlaff et al. (2025) results and moderators (which techniques reverse, effect sizes by expertise) were not retrieved.
- No direct comparison of prediction-first vs explanation-first at a 60-90 s scale, or for CS concepts specifically.
- The PF meta-analysis numbers and moderators remain unverified from the primary source.

## 3. Which single change would most likely beat "re-state the idea in a recap" at the End segment?

### Takeaway
Replace the recap with a cued-recall retrieval prompt (a short question the viewer answers before a pause, followed by the answer with a brief explanation). Retrieval beats restudy by about g = 0.33 in classrooms (Yang 2021) and g = 0.50 in the broader literature (Rowland 2014), with feedback adding benefit (g = 0.54 vs 0.37 in classrooms) and delayed tests showing larger gains. Caveat: restudy-style recap is rarely a pure "restudy" control, and effects against elaborative strategies were not significant.

### Cited Findings
- Classroom quizzing vs restudy g = 0.330 (the most conservative proxy for "recap"); vs no activity g = 0.610; vs elaborative strategies g = 0.095 (n.s.). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Testing vs restudy g = 0.50 overall (Rowland 2014); initial cued recall g = 0.61, free recall g = 0.29, recognition g = 0.29 (cued recall confounded with feedback). — [Rowland 2014](https://courseware.epfl.ch/assets/courseware/v1/fdde2f0aa590bf3b1324077a6bf1540c/asset-v1%3AEPFL%2BDEMO%2B2020%2Btype%40asset%2Bblock/Rowland2014-meta-analysis.pdf)
- Feedback after quizzing: g = 0.537 vs 0.374 without (Yang 2021); g = 0.73 vs 0.39 (Rowland 2014); no difference in Adesope 2017 (0.63 vs 0.60). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Quizzing benefits transfer to untested material (g = 0.321) and to rephrased questions (g = 0.558). — [Yang et al. 2021](https://gwern.net/docs/spacedrepetition/2021-yang.pdf)
- Matching format between practice test and final test increases the effect. — [Adesope 2017 summaries](https://www.tieonline.com/article/2232/the-benefits-of-practice-testing)
- In video lectures, embedded tests reduced mind-wandering and aided retention (Szpunar et al. 2013) but the effect was not robust in a 40-minute lecture (Jing et al. 2016) and later results are mixed. — [PMC3631699](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3631699/)
- Dunlosky et al. 2013 rated practice testing high utility and rereading low utility. — [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/releases/which-study-strategies-make-the-grade.html)
- Weaker alternatives to compare: self-explanation prompt g = 0.55 (Bisra 2018); teaching-expectancy "explain it to a friend" g = 0.35 (prepare) to 0.56 (teach) (Kobayashi 2019, snippet).

### Inferences
- Quantified expectation: replacing a recap with a retrieval prompt plus feedback is worth roughly g = 0.3 to 0.5 over restudy-like recap in the literature, with a larger gap at a 1+ day delay. The estimate is for learning of the practiced content; it will not be that large for unpracticed content (g about 0.3).
- Design: ask for recall of the core idea in the viewer's own words (cued recall, not recognition), leave a visible 3-5 s silent pause, then reveal an elaborated answer with a signaled key phrase. This puts feedback in the video itself and leaves the follow-up quiz to deliver the spaced repetition.
- Because the check question after the video is itself retrieval, a retrieval prompt in the End segment risks duplicating it. A way to separate them: use the End prompt to retrieve the idea, and make the follow-up check test a transfer variant.
- Compare against "self-explanation of the worked example" and "teach-back" as variants; each is plausible but has fewer classroom-scale data points for micro-videos.

### Gaps
- No study was found that directly compares a recap vs a retrieval prompt inside a 60-90 s educational video with a delayed test.
- Rowland's feedback figures were verified only through Yang et al.'s citation of them, not by locating them in Rowland's own text.
- Whether an unanswered "pause and think" (no overt response) gets the same retrieval benefit as an overt response was not found.

## 4. Does a curiosity "hook" at the start help learning, or only engagement?

### Takeaway
The evidence is thin and mixed. Lab work shows curiosity states enhance memory for the curiosity-linked answer and for incidental material encountered during curiosity; the one educational-video study found that curiosity could be induced but its link to learning was weak and inconsistent. A hook that is a guess-the-answer prequestion has real support (guessing g = 0.65) because of attention to the answer, not because of curiosity as such.

### Cited Findings
- Loewenstein (1994) information-gap theory: curiosity arises from a gap between what one knows and wants to know [SNIPPET, practitioner summaries]; popular statistics (28% better retention, "30% improvement" from a 40-study meta-analysis) have no traceable primary source and should not be used. — [Information Gap Theory summary](https://concepts.dsebastien.net/concept/information-gap-theory/)
- Gruber, Gelman & Ranganath (2014, Neuron): high-curiosity states improved memory for the curiosity-evoking answers and for incidental material; associated with dopaminergic/caudate activation. Kang et al. (2009) is the origin of the trivia paradigm. [SNIPPET] — [Cardiff record](https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/96033); [Learning & Memory, temporal proximity](https://learnmem.cshlp.org/content/28/2/34.full)
- Ho et al. (2021, Frontiers in Medicine): online videos induced curiosity in fifth-year medical students, but the relation with short-term learning outcomes was weak and inconsistent in size and direction (small sample, not generalizable). [SNIPPET] — [Frontiers in Medicine](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmed.2021.772956/pdf)
- Yeung-lab study (Oxford, 2026 publication): curiosity depends on whether learners perceive the answer as findable; higher availability of candidate answers predicted curiosity, moderate confidence and better memory; replicated in a second experiment controlling prior knowledge. [SNIPPET] — [Oxford record](https://www.psy.ox.ac.uk/publication/2440158)
- Prequestion meta-analysis: curiosity precursor account predicted larger effects for guessers and lower prior knowledge, consistent with the data; the effect hinges on the learner noting the answer in the study material. — [St. Hilaire et al. 2024](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10511812)
- No educational meta-analysis of curiosity hooks vs no hook on delayed retention or transfer was found.

### Inferences
- A hook helps learning mainly when it is a concrete question the video then answers (prequestion with guess), and when the learner perceives the answer as attainable. A generic narrative or "surprising fact" hook is supported mostly for engagement.
- Keep the hook short (a few seconds), tied to the exact target concept, and make the answer findable in the video.
- Test it: A/B the hook as (a) no hook, (b) curiosity story, (c) guess-the-answer prequestion, and measure the 7-day quiz.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed field evidence for hook vs no hook on delayed retention or transfer in educational videos.
- Effect sizes for curiosity-learning links in classrooms were not found; the repeated "28%" and "30%" claims are unsourced.
- Kang 2009 and Gruber 2014 details come from search summaries only.
